88 FATALITIES JACKSCREW THREAD STRIPPING NTSB/AAR-02/01 DCA00MA023 JANUARY 31, 2000 MCDONNELL DOUGLAS MD-83

Alaska Airlines Flight 261 — In-Flight Failure of the Horizontal Stabilizer Jackscrew Assembly

A Comprehensive Technical Report on Mechanical Wear, Inadequate Maintenance Lubrication, and Inverted Recovery Attempt over Anacapa Island.

Executive Overview: On January 31, 2000, a McDonnell Douglas MD-83 (N963AS) operating as Alaska Airlines Flight 261 crashed into the Pacific Ocean approximately 2.7 miles north of Anacapa Island, California. The aircraft suffered a total loss of pitch control when the Acme nut threads of the horizontal stabilizer trim system jackscrew stripped out completely due to excessive wear and lack of effective lubrication. Despite heroic efforts by the flight crew to maintain control, including rolling the aircraft inverted to counteract the violent dive, all 88 occupants (83 passengers and 5 crew members) were killed on impact.

Technical Breakdown & Root Cause Analysis

1. Mechanical Principles of the MD-80 Jackscrew Assembly

The MD-80 horizontal stabilizer trim system consists of an acme screw mounted inside the vertical stabilizer, driven by electric motors through a stationary Acme nut made of soft aluminum-bronze alloy:

  • Single Point Failure Mode: The design assumed that regular thread inspections and lubrication would prevent complete thread loss. Consequently, no mechanical fail-safe (such as a redundant follower nut or mechanical stop) was included in the original certification.
  • Thread Stripping Mechanism: Approximately 90% of the Acme nut threads had worn away prior to the flight due to chronic lack of lubrication. During flight, aerodynamic loads sheared the remaining 10% of threads, causing the screw to slip free until its lower stop slammed into the nut housing.
  • Torque Tube Fracture: Repeated dynamic hammering of the lower stop against the nut fractured the inner drive torque tube via low-cycle fatigue. This allowed the entire assembly to disconnect, driving the horizontal stabilizer far beyond its aerodynamic design limits.

2. Maintenance Practices and Cost-Cutting Factors

The NTSB highlighted severe systemic breakdowns in Alaska Airlines' maintenance program and FAA oversight:

  • Extended Inspection Intervals: Between 1985 and 1996, Alaska Airlines extended the lubrication interval and the "end-play check" (wear measurement) interval from 2,000 hours to much longer C-check intervals without adequate technical justification.
  • Inadequate End-Play Checks: Alaska Airlines utilized non-standard, custom-built measurement tools during end-play inspections that yielded inaccurate wear readings, masking the advanced destruction of the Acme nut.
  • Improper Grease Usage: Alaska Airlines substituted Mobilgrease 28 (clay-based) with Aeroshell Grease 33 (lithium-soap based) without proper compatibility testing. Combining incompatible greases caused breakdown in lubrication qualities, accelerating wear.

NTSB Probable Cause & Regulatory Legacy

  • Probable Cause: A loss of airplane pitch control resulting from the in-flight failure of the horizontal stabilizer trim system jackscrew assembly's Acme nut threads. The thread failure was caused by excessive wear resulting from Alaska Airlines' insufficient lubrication of the jackscrew assembly.
  • Redesigned Jackscrews: NASA and commercial aviation engineers designed a fail-safe redundant follower nut that automatically catches axial loads if the primary Acme nut fails.
  • Strict Maintenance Oversight: The FAA revised Airworthiness Directives (AD 2000-15-15) mandating mandatory end-play inspections every 2,000 flight hours using validated measurement fixtures.
  • Pilot Heroism: Captain Ted Thompson and First Officer Bill Tansky were posthumously awarded the ALPA Gold Medal for Heroism for their extraordinary skill and courage in attempting to save the aircraft.
